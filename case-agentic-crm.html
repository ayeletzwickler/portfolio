Agentic CRM

The first agentic CRM in the US automotive industry, built around AI agents from day one. In its first full dealership deployment it handled 24 leads in a week, set 5 appointments with no manual outreach, and ran a full team migration without a single missed sale.

Role

Product Designer (End-to-End)

Team

CPO, COO, PM, Engineering

Timeline

Oct 2025 – current

Impact

Acquired by COX Automotive · still in BETA

Why we built it

Three reasons we stopped syncing to someone else’s CRM.

01

Dealerships kept asking.

Multiple clients, over time, making the same case. They wanted a CRM that understood what the agents were doing – not one they had to explain it to.

02

We already had the data.

Purchase history, engagement signals, equity position – all in the system. We were routing it through third-party CRMs and maintaining a bridge to make the agents useful. Building around what we owned made more sense.

03

The window was open.

AI made it possible to build in weeks what would have taken a year. Nothing like this existed in the automotive CRM space. We moved.

October 2025

Three people, two days, one prototype.

The product didn’t start with a spec.

Me, the CPO, and the COO took two days offsite with one question: could we actually build this? We built a prototype, walked into a dealership, and showed it to a GM. The feedback was direct: what was missing, what felt wrong, what they actually needed.

Three roles, two layers of design problem.

Sales Rep

The Sales Rep

spent their day switching between a CRM, a texting platform, and their phone. Follow-ups were manual, context got lost between tools, and first contact on new leads was slow. The opportunity: an AI that drafts the message in their voice, surfaces the right context at the right moment, and keeps them in control of every send.

The old pain was broken workflows.

The new pain was getting people to hand over work to something they’d never used before.

Key decisions

Progressive agentic, not pure AI. The reps we observed weren’t going to work out of a chat interface. They needed something that looked like a CRM, with AI embedded inside it, not an AI that happened to have a CRM attached. Drafts appear in a familiar inbox-style queue. The rep approves, edits, or ignores. Trust builds incrementally.

The customer, not the deal, is the atomic unit. Legacy CRMs organize everything around transactions. Fullpath already held a customer data platform with full histories, engagement signals, and equity data. Building around the customer meant every agent had the right context before opening a conversation. Reps stopped treating the same person as a new lead every six months.

Mobile is for reply, not management. A lead that waits ten minutes goes cold. Managers wanted a dashboard on their phone. What reps actually needed was a way to respond from the lot. The mobile app does one thing well: the active conversation thread, in under two taps.

Agents as visible teammates, not hidden settings. Hiding the AI in a settings menu solves adoption the wrong way. Each agent has a name, a defined role, and visible limits. Draft cards show up inline with a clear signal of what triggered them. The rep always knows what the agent did and why.

Four bets we made before we had a roadmap:

Bet 01

Progressive agentic, not pure AI.

Leadership wanted agents to handle everything. I pushed for familiar workflows with AI embedded inside them. Users who’ve spent a decade in the same CRM won’t adapt to “just ask the AI.” Trust builds gradually.

In the UI: the home screen surfaces a queue of AI-drafted messages for rep approval, a familiar inbox rather than a chat interface.

Screenshot TK

Home screen inbox queue

Bet 02

Customer as atomic unit.

Most CRMs organize around deals. We organized around the person. Fullpath’s CDP knows who the customer is before any interaction: purchase history, vehicle, website behavior, equity position, at the person level.

In the UI: the primary record is a person card with the relationship timeline, vehicle context, and full conversation history. The deal is a secondary tab.

Screenshot TK

Bet 03

Mobile = reply, not manage.

Reps don’t manage from their phone. They pull it out because a customer just texted. The entire mobile IA is built around speed of reply.

In the UI: mobile opens to active conversations. Reply is the first action. No dashboard. Just what needs a response now.

Screenshot TK

Bet 04

Agents as visible teammates.

Four agents with names, roles, and clear limits: Omni (co-writer), Task Builder (event-driven briefings), Pipeline Agent (lead routing), Lead Handling (first contact ant sets appointments). Not hidden features. Visible colleagues.

In the UI: agents have named presence inline. Omni shows a draft card in the rep’s home screen, not a notification badge. You see what the agent prepared before you see the outcome.

Designing the Human-AI Relationship

Building an Agentic CRM meant solving a specific UX tension: sales reps feared the AI would damage customer relationships; managers feared reps wouldn’t adopt it. Both fears are legitimate. The product only works if reps trust it enough to let it act, and that trust has to be earned gradually, not assumed. I established three UI patterns to hold that balance.

Pattern 01

Inline drafts, not autonomous actions. Omni’s output lives in the rep’s action queue, a draft card they see before anything goes anywhere. One tap to Approve, Edit, or Regenerate. The rep is always the last step. This kept humans in the loop while eliminating the blank-page problem that slows first contact.

Pattern 02

Context transparency: the AI shows its work. Every agent recommendation surfaces the reasoning behind it: the triggering event (“Customer viewed 2023 Civic 3× today”), live inventory status (“Stock #4821, Available”), and a confidence signal. The rep sees what the agent sees before deciding whether to act on it. This was the fix for the Fisher Honda problem. A rep who can verify in-context doesn’t need to trust blindly.

Pattern 03

Explicit handoff. When a rep steps in to write manually or takes a call, the UI shifts from Agent Handling to Rep Owned, with a visible status badge. The rep knows, the manager knows, the next shift knows. No duplicate outreach, no silent collisions between agent and human.

November 2025 — First rep, one week in a dealership

One rep, real leads, real customers, real calls.

Found: No stock numbers or VINs on vehicle cards. No sold badge. Dialer couldn’t be minimized during calls. Notification bell too small to tap. Real-time messaging didn’t update without refresh. Small things. Together: the product didn’t feel like it was built for someone who actually sells cars.

Changed: Unified customer timeline. Inventory transparency layer: VINs, sold badges, availability status. Call window rebuilt to minimize without losing context. Notifications overhauled.

Design principle: if a rep has to leave the screen to verify something basic, the AI’s output is already suspect. Every fix was about closing the gap between what the system knew and what the rep could see without looking elsewhere.

January 2026 — Second iteration

The November fixes held, but a new layer of friction surfaced around handoffs and task context. Back inside the dealership to pressure-test what we’d changed. We refined, went back the following week, and got closer to something a rep could rely on without thinking about it.

February 2026 — NADA: first agentic CRM in the industry

We used NADA to announce the first agentic CRM in the automotive industry, and the response was immediate. NADA is one of the largest automotive industry conferences in the US. We ran demos for hundreds of potential customers. The product hadn’t fully launched, but the industry recognized the gap we were filling. It created the kind of momentum you can’t manufacture.

March 2026 — Fisher Honda: two reps, real accountability

Back in a dealership with a harder bar: the product needed to work without us in the room.

Found: 22 pending notifications had piled up. Omni offered a customer pictures of a car that had already been sold. Suggested a purchase path to someone who’d come in only for a trade-in. The AI was doing things, but it wasn’t reading the room.

“If it can email and text a customer and I can sell cars, I don’t care.”

Franklin, Sales Rep @ Fisher Honda (3 days into pilot)

Sentiment: 9/10. Asked to keep his login. 24 leads worked, 47 tasks clicked, 22 completed, 13 activities, 5 appointments set.

Changed: Omni’s context engine rewired: no suggesting sold cars, no purchase path for a trade-in customer, no acting without full history. Szott Ford, a concurrent early dealership, reinforced the same lesson: AI released too early, felt cold, UX too complex. We pulled it back, tightened persona definitions, rebuilt onboarding before re-engaging.

Design insight: AI errors in a sales context aren’t just bad UX. They’re trust-breaking moments that take weeks to repair. The response had to be preventive, not corrective. Omni now surfaces relevant context inline before drafting: the customer’s last intent signal, active inventory match, prior conversation summary. The rep sees what the AI sees before it acts.

May 2026 — Iteration

Refinements ahead of broader rollout: pipeline labels adapted to match dealer floor language, mobile media handling for lot photos, manual customer entry for walk-ins, sales rep scorecard. The product was getting harder to replicate from a spec.

May brought a new agent: the pipeline agent, which replaces the most expensive manual task in a manager’s morning. In legacy CRMs, lead distribution is a manual morning task. Every manager sorts through the day’s leads and assigns them to reps by hand, weighing performance and capacity, before anyone on the floor makes a single call. The pipeline agent takes that off their plate. Managers configure the rules once (rep performance thresholds, lead type priorities, capacity limits) and the agent handles the daily distribution. It’s one of the clearest cases in the product of what an agent should actually do: a repetitive, judgment-based workflow that belongs to the manager conceptually but costs them real time every day.

June 2026 — COX Automotive acquires Fullpath

The product led to Fullpath’s acquisition by COX Automotive, one of the largest automotive technology companies in the US.

July–August 2026 — First full dealership transition

The real test wasn’t one rep. It was an entire dealership switching from their existing CRM to ours and continuing to sell. Full two-month migration. Every sales rep moved to the system exclusively. Complete.

July and August also meant shipping fast. Alongside the migration, we pushed seven significant features in those eight weeks.

Conversations screen: AI-pending drafts and overdue threads surface first. [visual: conversations screen] Reps needed a single view of every active thread, sorted in a way that told them where to focus. We settled on a hybrid: threads where the AI had a draft waiting and threads with an overdue response rise to the top.

Task page and task panel: tasks got their own screen, but needed to stay accessible without breaking the conversation. [visual: task page and task panel] Tasks had lived inside conversation threads. Giving them their own page exposed a second problem: reps didn’t want to leave a conversation to manage its tasks. The inline panel kept tasks accessible without a context switch.

Notifications: we flipped from reps checking in to the system pushing, with manager control over noise. [visual: notifications] The first version relied on reps checking in proactively. The constraint was noise: too many alerts and reps would turn them off. We gave managers control over what triggers a notification rather than defaulting to everything.

Add flows: getting a lead or customer into the CRM went from a multi-step detour to under thirty seconds. [visual: add flows] The add flow uses a bottom sheet that pre-fills from existing data.

Customer page: a full picture of who a rep was about to call so they could walk in prepared. [visual: customer page] The customer page gave reps conversation history, vehicle interest, prior interactions, and what the AI agents had done on that account. Everything in one place.

Managers overview: a configurable dashboard so each manager sees the numbers they actually care about. [visual: managers overview] Customizable widgets across rep activity, AI performance, lead pipeline, and follow-up coverage.

iOS app: the design question wasn’t what to show on mobile, it was what to leave out. [visual: iOS app screens] Managers had been asking for mobile access since October. A desktop that fits in your pocket is a bad app. We gave them a live view of agent and rep activity and nothing more.

September 2026 — Beta: second dealership live

A second dealership began beta. Onboarding in progress.

What we track: message reply rate versus pre-agent baseline, task completion rate per rep, appointment conversion from AI-suggested outreach, time-to-first-contact on new leads. The July–August full-dealership migration is the most direct signal: not a pilot, not a cherry-picked rep, but an entire sales floor switched over and selling.

The October 2025 decisions are still holding. Not because they were right on paper, but because they survived contact with the sales floor.

2027 — Migration at scale

COX runs on VinSolutions, a legacy CRM with churn that reflects how the product has aged. Dealerships know it hasn’t kept up technically, and they’re looking for a way out.

The main target for 2027 is a full data migration: move COX’s customer history, lead data, and conversation records into Fullpath’s system, then shift users off VinSolutions onto ours. We’ve done this at one dealership. The next question is whether we can do it at scale, across COX’s network, without breaking sales operations in the process.

Running parallel: continuing to build out the product so it’s ready for that volume. The data has to move cleanly. The rep experience has to feel like a real step forward. A migration that works technically but leaves people missing their old tool is not a win.
