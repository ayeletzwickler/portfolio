Agentic CRM

Fullpath built its AI agents before it had a CRM. I led the design of that CRM end to end, web and native mobile, four AI agents, three user types, 0 to 1. It started as a two-day offsite prototype and ended in a COX Automotive acquisition.

Timeline

Oct 2025 to present

Role

Product Designer, end to end

Team

CPO, COO, PM, Engineering

Impact

Acquired by COX Automotive. Beta. Two dealerships live.

Why we built it

Fullpath had AI agents working before it had a CRM.

The agents handled leads, set appointments, and followed up with shoppers. But all of that work surfaced inside legacy systems Fullpath did not own and could not shape. We built the CRM to close that gap. Not to replicate what existed, but to build the first place where AI agents and the people managing them could work as one team.

Three things converged at the same time.

01

Organic demand. Dealerships kept asking, multiple clients, over time, making the same case. They wanted a CRM that understood what the agents were doing.

02

Data we already held. A significant portion of each dealership's customer data was already in the system. Building around it made more sense than routing it through another platform.

03

A rare technology window. AI made it possible to build in weeks what would have taken a year before. Nothing like this existed in automotive, and the window was open.

We did not bolt AI onto an old system. We built a CRM that is agentic from day one, designed around the data Fullpath already owned and the agents already at work.

How it started

Three people, two days, one prototype.

In October 2025, me, the CPO, and the COO took two days offsite with one question: could we actually build this?

We did not write a spec. We built a prototype, walked into a dealership, and showed it to a GM. The feedback was direct: what was missing, what felt wrong, what they actually needed on a Tuesday morning. You cannot get that from a requirements doc.

That session shaped the first six months of the product. In a 0 to 1 build, the fastest way to reduce risk is to make something real. The prototype was not a shortcut. It was the most important thing we could have done first.

The users

Three roles, two layers of pain.

The old pain was broken workflows with legacy tools. The new pain was harder: how do you make people trust AI they have never used?

A dealership runs on three roles. Each one has a different relationship with the CRM, and each one will have a different relationship with AI.

Screenshot TK

Sales rep home: today's leads, calls and tasks, with the agent's draft cards visible alongside.

User 01

The sales rep lives in the short term. They want to sell a car today.

Their day is leads, calls, follow-ups, appointments.

The old pain. Manual work everywhere: copying AI conversation summaries into the CRM, checking vehicle availability in a separate system, figuring out which lead to call next.

The new pain. An AI agent is now talking to their customers. When do they step in? How do they know what the agent said? Can they trust it not to damage a deal they have been working on?

Screenshot TK

Sales manager overview: rep activity, lead routing, and agent handling status side by side.

User 02

The sales manager sits between the reps and the business.

The old pain. They are the manual bridge between everything. When the AI sets an appointment, the manager schedules it for the rep. When a customer reschedules with the agent, the rep has no way to know.

The new pain. They know what their team did. They have no visibility into what the AI did on the team's behalf. How do they maintain oversight over agents they did not configure and conversations they did not read?

Screenshot TK

GM dashboard: dealership performance, agent activity log, and conversion by source.

User 03

The GM cares about the dealership as a whole.

Revenue, performance, whether the technology investment is paying off.

The old pain. Zero visibility into what is actually happening on the floor.

The new pain. They are being asked to activate AI agents they cannot see working. No trust signal before they flip the switch. They are accountable for results they cannot audit.

This is the design problem at two levels. First, replace broken workflows with something better. Second and harder, make people who have never used AI trust it enough to let it into their daily work. A CRM that solves only the first problem is a better tool. A CRM that solves both is a new way of working.

Key decisions

Four bets we made before we had a roadmap.

Screenshot TK

Sales rep home: the AI draft cards and familiar pipeline structure coexisting.

Decision 01

Progressive agentic, not pure AI.

Leadership wanted agents to handle everything. I pushed for familiar workflows with AI embedded inside them.

Users who have spent a decade in the same CRM will not adapt to "just ask the AI." Trust builds gradually. In the UI, the home screen surfaces a queue of AI-drafted messages for rep approval. A familiar inbox, not a chat interface.

Screenshot TK

Customer page: the primary record is a person card with relationship timeline, vehicle context, and full conversation history.

Decision 02

Customer as atomic unit, not deal as atomic unit.

Most automotive CRMs organize around deals. We organized around the person.

Fullpath's CDP knows who the customer is before any interaction: purchase history, vehicle, website behavior, equity position, all at the person level. Organizing around deals would mean ignoring the company's biggest competitive advantage.

In the UI, the primary record is a person card with a relationship timeline, vehicle context, and full conversation history. The deal is a secondary tab.

Screenshot TK

Mobile app: the primary tab is Chats, not a dashboard. Reply is the first action on every screen.

Decision 03

Mobile is for reply, not for managing.

After spending time with reps on the dealership floor, one pattern was impossible to miss. They do not use their phone to manage their day. They pull it out because a customer just sent a message.

A lead that waits 10 minutes is cold. So the primary tab in our mobile app is Chats. Not Tasks, not Pipeline, not Home. The entire mobile IA is built around speed of reply. Desktop is where you plan. Mobile is where you stay in the conversation.

Screenshot TK

Agents Cafe: four named agents with their roles, status indicators, and handoff controls visible to the rep.

Decision 04

Agents as visible teammates, not hidden features.

The four agents have names, roles, and clear limits. Not settings buried in a config panel. Visible colleagues with named presence inline.

Omni shows a draft card in the rep's home screen, not a notification badge. You see what the agent prepared before you see the outcome. This was not a cosmetic decision. Making agent activity visible was the primary mechanism for building trust.

Human-AI design

The hardest problem was not the technology.

Building an agentic CRM meant solving a specific tension: sales reps feared the AI would damage customer relationships. Managers feared reps would not adopt it. Both fears are legitimate.

The product only works if reps trust it enough to let it act, and that trust has to be earned gradually. I established three UI patterns to hold that balance.

Pattern 01

Inline drafts, not autonomous actions.

Omni's output lives in the rep's action queue, a draft card they see before anything goes anywhere. One tap to Approve, Edit, or Regenerate. The rep is always the last step. This kept humans in the loop while eliminating the blank-page problem that slows first contact.

Pattern 02

Context transparency. The AI shows its work.

Every agent recommendation surfaces the reasoning behind it: the triggering event ("Customer viewed 2023 Civic 3x today"), live inventory status ("Stock 4821, available"), and a confidence signal. The rep sees what the agent sees before deciding whether to act on it.

This was the fix for the Fisher Honda problem. A rep who can verify in-context does not need to trust blindly. AI errors in a sales context are not just bad UX. They are trust-breaking moments that take weeks to repair. The response has to be preventive, not corrective.

Pattern 03

Explicit handoff. Everyone knows who owns the conversation.

When a rep steps in to write manually or takes a call, the UI shifts from Agent Handling to Rep Owned, with a visible status badge. The rep knows, the manager knows, the next shift knows. No duplicate outreach, no silent collisions between agent and human.

Field research

We went back every time we thought we were done.

Every major iteration started inside a dealership. Not with a survey, not with usability testing in a controlled environment. With real reps, real leads, real customers, and real calls.

Nov 2025

First rep. One week embedded.

Found: no stock numbers or VINs on vehicle cards. No sold badge. The dialer could not be minimized during calls. Notification bell too small to tap. Real-time messaging did not update without refresh.

Small things. Together, they made the product feel like it was not built for someone who actually sells cars.

Changed: unified customer timeline. Inventory transparency layer with VINs, sold badges, and availability status. Call window rebuilt to minimize without losing context. Notifications overhauled.

Design principle: if a rep has to leave the screen to verify something basic, the AI's output is already suspect.

Jan 2026

Second iteration. Back to validate the fixes.

The November fixes held, but a new layer of friction surfaced around handoffs and task context. We refined, returned the following week, and got closer to something a rep could rely on without thinking about it.

Feb 2026

NADA: the industry recognized the gap before the product fully launched.

NADA is one of the largest automotive industry conferences in the US. We announced the first agentic CRM in the industry and ran demos for hundreds of potential customers. The product was not fully live, but the response was immediate. It created the kind of momentum you cannot manufacture.

Mar 2026

Fisher Honda. Two reps. Real accountability.

The product needed to work without us in the room. Back in a dealership with a harder bar.

Found: 22 pending notifications piled up. Omni offered a customer pictures of a car that had already been sold. Suggested a purchase path to someone who came in only for a trade-in. The AI was doing things, but it was not reading the room.

"If it can email and text a customer and I can sell cars, I don't care."

Franklin, Sales Rep at Fisher Honda (3 days into pilot)

Franklin's sentiment: 9 out of 10. He asked to keep his login. In one week: 24 leads worked, 47 tasks clicked, 22 completed, 13 activities, 5 appointments set.

Changed: Omni's context engine rewired. No suggesting sold cars. No purchase path for a trade-in customer. No acting without full history. Omni now surfaces relevant context inline before drafting: the customer's last intent signal, active inventory match, prior conversation summary. The rep sees what the AI sees before it acts.

May 2026

Pipeline agent launched. The most expensive manual task, automated.

In legacy CRMs, lead distribution is a manual morning task. Every manager sorts through the day's leads and assigns them to reps by hand, weighing performance and capacity, before anyone on the floor makes a single call.

The pipeline agent takes that off their plate. Managers configure the rules once: rep performance thresholds, lead type priorities, capacity limits. The agent handles the daily distribution. It is one of the clearest cases in the product of what an agent should actually do: a repetitive, judgment-based workflow that belongs to the manager conceptually but costs them real time every day.

The agents

Four agents, each with a clear job.

The CRM ships with four AI agents. Each one has a defined role, a visible presence in the UI, and a clear boundary between what it handles and what the human handles. They are presented as teammates, not features and not settings.

01 — Omni

The rep's co-writer.

Drafts emails, SMS, and call talking points. Answers questions about customers, inventory, and performance. Always accessible from the home screen, from within a task, from the mobile overlay. The rep approves every send.

02 — Task Builder

Turns events into briefings.

Watches real-time signals and creates tasks from them. Not static cadences or day-1-day-3 reminders. A task says: "Call Jordan, texted back 4 minutes ago about the CR-V. Confirm stock and offer a Saturday test drive." A briefing, not a to-do.

03 — Pipeline Agent

Routes and ranks leads for managers.

Dealerships have complex rules for lead distribution: rep performance, availability, territory, round-robin with exceptions. Managers configure the rules once in plain language. The agent runs the morning distribution so managers start the day selling, not sorting.

04 — Lead Handling

Owns first contact.

Handles first contact with new leads autonomously. Responds within minutes, follows up, sets appointments. When it is time for a human, it hands off with full context: what it said, what the customer said, what comes next.

Franklin, the first rep at Fisher Honda, asked to keep his login after three days. The product led to Fullpath's acquisition by COX Automotive in June 2026, one of the largest automotive technology companies in the US.

What we track. Message reply rate versus the pre-agent baseline. Task completion rate per rep. Appointment conversion from AI-suggested outreach. Time-to-first-contact on new leads.

The July and August 2026 full-dealership migration is the most direct signal: not a pilot, not a single rep, but an entire sales floor switched over and continuing to sell. The seven features shipped in those eight weeks: the conversations screen, task page and panel, notification system, add flows, customer page, managers overview, and the iOS app.

What's next

Migration at scale. COX runs on a legacy CRM with churn.

COX Automotive runs on VinSolutions, a legacy CRM that has not kept up technically. Dealerships know it, and they are looking for a way out.

The main target for 2027 is a full data migration: move COX's customer history, lead data, and conversation records into Fullpath's system, then shift users off VinSolutions onto ours. We have done this at one dealership. The next question is whether we can do it at scale, across COX's network, without breaking sales operations in the process.

Running parallel: continuing to build out the product so it is ready for that volume. The data has to move cleanly. The rep experience has to feel like a real step forward. A migration that works technically but leaves people missing their old tool is not a win.

We have two dealerships in beta. The next chapter is proving the model holds when the numbers are an order of magnitude larger.

Questions

Things people ask me about this project.

What was your role? +

Design end to end, from the offsite prototype onward: the web platform and the native mobile app, four AI agents, three user types, 0 to 1. I was in every dealership visit and in every major product decision.

How did the project start? +

Three of us, me, the CPO and the COO, took two days offsite in October 2025. We did not write a spec. We built a prototype and walked into a dealership to show it to a GM. That session shaped the next six months. The feedback was direct: what was missing, what felt wrong, what they actually needed on a Tuesday morning.

Why not make it fully agentic from day one? +

Leadership wanted pure AI. I argued for progressive agentic instead: keep the pipeline, tasks, and customer page people have used for a decade, and let the agents work inside that structure. Strip out everything familiar and users lose trust before they ever see what the system can do. Trust builds gradually. The evidence from the dealership pilots confirmed this. Franklin at Fisher Honda was skeptical of AI. He asked to keep his login after three days.

Why organize around the customer instead of the deal? +

Because that is where Fullpath's data lives. The CDP knows the person before any interaction: purchase history, vehicle, website behavior, equity position. Organizing around deals would have meant ignoring the company's biggest competitive advantage. From user testing, reps responded to the customer-centered view immediately. It matched how they actually think about their work.

What was the hardest problem to solve? +

Getting the AI to earn trust rather than assume it. The Fisher Honda lesson: Omni offered a customer pictures of a car that had already been sold. That single mistake surfaced a systemic problem. The agent was acting on incomplete context. The fix was context transparency: every recommendation now surfaces the reasoning behind it before the rep decides to act. The rep sees what the AI sees. That shift, from "trust the agent's output" to "here is what the agent knows, you decide," changed how reps used the product.

How did the COX acquisition happen? +

The product and the momentum it created, especially after NADA in February 2026, put Fullpath on COX Automotive's radar. COX is one of the largest automotive technology companies in the US. The acquisition closed in June 2026. The 2027 focus is migrating dealerships off VinSolutions, COX's existing legacy CRM, onto Fullpath's platform at scale.

Where is it now? +

In beta. One dealership has completed a full two-month migration from its existing CRM, and a second is in onboarding. We shipped seven major features in July and August 2026 alongside that first migration. The product is still in motion, but the early decisions are holding.
