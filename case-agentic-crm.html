Agentic CRM

The first agentic CRM in the US automotive industry, built around AI agents from day one. In its first full dealership deployment it handled 24 leads in a week, set 5 appointments with no manual outreach, and ran a full team migration without a single missed sale.

Role

Product Designer (End-to-End)

Team

CPO, COO, PM, Engineering

Timeline

Oct 2025 – current

Impact

Acquired by COX Automotive · still in BETA

Challenge

Three reasons we stopped syncing to someone else’s CRM, and three roles we had to design for.

01

Dealerships kept asking.

Multiple clients, over time, making the same case. They wanted a CRM that understood what the agents were doing – not one they had to explain it to.

02

We already had the data.

Purchase history, engagement signals, equity position – all in the system. We were routing it through third-party CRMs and maintaining a bridge to make the agents useful. Building around what we owned made more sense.

03

The window was open.

AI made it possible to build in weeks what would have taken a year. Nothing like this existed in the automotive CRM space. We moved.

The product didn’t start with a spec. Me, the CPO, and the COO took two days offsite with one question: could we actually build this? We built a prototype, walked into a dealership, and showed it to a GM. The feedback was direct: what was missing, what felt wrong, what they actually needed.

That feedback split into three roles, each with its own design problem.

The Sales Rep

spent their day switching between a CRM, a texting platform, and their phone. Follow-ups were manual, context got lost between tools, and first contact on new leads was slow.

The opportunity

An AI that drafts the message in their voice, surfaces the right context at the right moment, and keeps them in control of every send.

Visual: Sales Rep view

The Sales Manager

had visibility into rep activity but not agent activity. They knew what their team did; they didn’t know what the AI was doing on their behalf.

The opportunity

A live view of agent and rep activity side by side, so managers can catch a problem before it lands rather than explain it after.

Visual: Sales Manager view

The GM

was asked to trust AI tools they couldn’t audit. Adoption decisions were based on vendor promises, not visible outcomes.

The opportunity

Accountability built into the product. The GM can see what the AI did, what converted, and what was handled manually, without chasing down reports.

Visual: GM view

The old pain was broken workflows. The new pain was getting people to hand over work to something they’d never used before.

Strategic bets

Four bets we made before we had a roadmap.

01

Progressive agentic, not pure AI.

Reps weren’t going to work out of a chat interface. They needed something that looked like a CRM with AI embedded inside. Drafts appear in a familiar queue. The rep approves, edits, or ignores. Trust builds incrementally.

02

The customer, not the deal, is the atomic unit.

Legacy CRMs organize around transactions. Fullpath already held full histories, engagement signals, and equity data. Building around the customer meant every agent had the right context before opening a conversation.

03

Mobile is for reply, not management.

A lead that waits ten minutes goes cold. What reps actually needed was a way to respond from the lot. The mobile app does one thing well: the active conversation thread, in under two taps.

04

Agents as visible teammates, not hidden settings.

Hiding the AI in a settings menu solves adoption the wrong way. Each agent has a name, a defined role, and visible limits. The rep always knows what the agent did and why.

Human-AI design patterns

Designing the Human-AI Relationship

Building an Agentic CRM meant solving a specific UX tension: sales reps feared the AI would damage customer relationships; managers feared reps wouldn’t adopt it. Both fears are legitimate. The product only works if reps trust it enough to let it act, and that trust has to be earned gradually, not assumed. I established three UI patterns to hold that balance.

Pattern 01

Inline drafts, not autonomous actions. Omni’s output lives in the rep’s action queue, a draft card they see before anything goes anywhere. One tap to Approve, Edit, or Regenerate. The rep is always the last step. This kept humans in the loop while eliminating the blank-page problem that slows first contact.

Pattern 02

Context transparency: the AI shows its work. Every agent recommendation surfaces the reasoning behind it: the triggering event (“Customer viewed 2023 Civic 3× today”), live inventory status (“Stock #4821, Available”), and a confidence signal. The rep sees what the agent sees before deciding whether to act on it. This was the fix for the Fisher Honda problem. A rep who can verify in-context doesn’t need to trust blindly.

Pattern 03

Explicit handoff. When a rep steps in to write manually or takes a call, the UI shifts from Agent Handling to Rep Owned, with a visible status badge. The rep knows, the manager knows, the next shift knows. No duplicate outreach, no silent collisions between agent and human.

Feature deep-dives

Five features that shipped alongside a full dealership migration.

01

Pipeline Agent

In legacy CRMs, lead distribution is a manual morning task. Every manager sorts through the day’s leads and assigns them to reps by hand, weighing performance and capacity, before anyone on the floor makes a single call. The pipeline agent takes that off their plate. Managers configure the rules once (rep performance thresholds, lead type priorities, capacity limits) and the agent handles the daily distribution. It’s one of the clearest cases in the product of what an agent should actually do: a repetitive, judgment-based workflow that belongs to the manager conceptually but costs them real time every day.

02

Conversations Screen

Reps needed a single view of every active thread, sorted in a way that told them where to focus. We settled on a hybrid: threads where the AI had a draft waiting and threads with an overdue response rise to the top.

03

Customer Page

A full picture of who a rep was about to call so they could walk in prepared. The customer page gave reps conversation history, vehicle interest, prior interactions, and what the AI agents had done on that account. Everything in one place.

04

Managers Overview

A configurable dashboard so each manager sees the numbers they actually care about. Customizable widgets across rep activity, AI performance, lead pipeline, and follow-up coverage.

05

iOS App

The design question wasn’t what to show on mobile, it was what to leave out. Managers had been asking for mobile access since October. A desktop that fits in your pocket is a bad app. We gave them a live view of agent and rep activity and nothing more.

Obstacles

The Fisher Honda moment.

Back in a dealership with a harder bar: the product needed to work without us in the room.

Found: 22 pending notifications had piled up. Omni offered a customer pictures of a car that had already been sold. Suggested a purchase path to someone who’d come in only for a trade-in. The AI was doing things, but it wasn’t reading the room.

“If it can email and text a customer and I can sell cars, I don’t care.”

Franklin, Sales Rep @ Fisher Honda (3 days into pilot)

Sentiment: 9/10. Asked to keep his login. 24 leads worked, 47 tasks clicked, 22 completed, 13 activities, 5 appointments set.

Changed: Omni’s context engine rewired: no suggesting sold cars, no purchase path for a trade-in customer, no acting without full history. Szott Ford, a concurrent early dealership, reinforced the same lesson: AI released too early, felt cold, UX too complex. We pulled it back, tightened persona definitions, rebuilt onboarding before re-engaging.

Design insight: AI errors in a sales context aren’t just bad UX. They’re trust-breaking moments that take weeks to repair. The response had to be preventive, not corrective. Omni now surfaces relevant context inline before drafting: the customer’s last intent signal, active inventory match, prior conversation summary. The rep sees what the AI sees before it acts.

Outcomes

What shipped, and what’s next.

24

Leads worked in Fisher Honda’s first week, no manual outreach

5

Appointments set from that same week

1

Acquisition – COX Automotive acquired Fullpath

1

Full dealership migration, two months, zero missed sales

We used NADA, one of the largest automotive industry conferences in the US, to announce the first agentic CRM in the industry. The product hadn’t fully launched, but the industry recognized the gap we were filling. It created the kind of momentum you can’t manufacture.

A second dealership is now in beta. What we track: message reply rate versus pre-agent baseline, task completion rate per rep, appointment conversion from AI-suggested outreach, time-to-first-contact on new leads. The full-dealership migration remains the most direct signal: not a pilot, not a cherry-picked rep, but an entire sales floor switched over and selling. The October 2025 decisions are still holding, not because they were right on paper, but because they survived contact with the sales floor.

2027: migration at scale. COX runs on VinSolutions, a legacy CRM that dealerships know hasn’t kept up technically. The main target for 2027 is a full data migration: move COX’s customer history, lead data, and conversation records into Fullpath’s system, then shift users off VinSolutions onto ours. We’ve done this at one dealership. The next question is whether we can do it at scale, across COX’s network, without breaking sales operations in the process. Running parallel: continuing to build out the product so it’s ready for that volume. A migration that works technically but leaves people missing their old tool is not a win.
